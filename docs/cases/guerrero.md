## What Confidential Records Hide Here

- status split for confidential records
- date of disappearance
- date of report/registration
- sex
- birth date / age
- municipality

Interpretation rule: confidential records still count as unresolved people, but they
cannot be used for year, sex, age, municipality, or status-specific analysis.

## Evidence Checklist

### Local Search/Fiscalia Rules About Publication

Question: does the state have rules, protocols, notices, or registry language explaining
what gets published, withheld, anonymized, or treated as confidential?

Status: VERIFIED (2026-07-06).

- Guerrero's Comisión Estatal de Búsqueda de Personas (CEBP) publishes a privacy notice
  (https://www.guerrero.gob.mx/convocatoria/aviso-de-privacidad-cebp/) implementing the
  **same federal consent architecture** as everywhere else: search bulletins are
  disseminated "con autorización previa"; families may restrict data to
  search-and-identification use only or request confidentiality "por razones de
  seguridad". Legal basis cited: CPEUM arts. 6 & 16, state data-protection Law 466,
  federal data laws, Ley General en Materia de Desaparición.
- Implication for the contrast: the *rules* in Guerrero and Jalisco are essentially the
  same. What differs is capture practice — which is exactly what
  [../opacity-mechanism.md](../opacity-mechanism.md) predicts drives the confidential
  share. Guerrero has no documented blanket-confidentiality policy equivalent to
  Jalisco's (CNB oficio 1349/2020 names Jalisco, not Guerrero).
- A further candidate driver of LOW redaction (interpretation, not yet directly
  verified): a large share of Guerrero's records are historic forced-disappearance
  cases (Guerra Sucia, Ayotzinapa) entered via federal/CNDH/COMVERDAD channels. The
  same CNB oficio argues that for grave human-rights violations the SCJN's máxima
  publicidad doctrine applies (Segunda Sala AR 911/2016) and names **COMVERDAD
  Guerrero** among its integration sources — i.e., a whole class of Guerrero records
  where confidentiality is legally *disfavored*.

### Official Statements About Privacy/Redaction

Question: has the state justified confidentiality as privacy, victim protection, child
protection, investigation integrity, or another reason?

Status: VERIFIED (2026-07-06), with a null result that is itself informative: no
Guerrero-specific official statement defending or explaining registry redaction was
found — unlike Jalisco, Guerrero has not needed to deny manipulating its figures. The
CEBP privacy notice (above) is the only official publication-policy language located.
The state's registry controversy is about *under-registration and inaction*, not
redaction.

### Collective Or Journalist Criticism

Question: have families, collectives, journalists, NGOs, or transparency advocates
criticized registry opacity, undercounting, reclassification, or deleted records in this state?

Status: VERIFIED (2026-07-06). Criticism is intense but targets **omission, not
redaction** — the mirror image of Jalisco:

- **Tlachinollan** ("Guerrero: territorio de personas desaparecidas",
  https://www.tlachinollan.org/guerrero-territorio-de-personas-desaparecidas/): the
  state registers ~3,800 disappeared while collectives document far more — in Iguala
  alone three collectives jointly account for 623 cases. Fiscalía investigations
  "stalled" unless families force reopening; forensic services collapsed (they demand a
  regional human-identification center); 2–6% prosecution rate, 36 sentences nationally.
- Local prosecutors refusing to take reports and family distrust are named drivers of
  RNPDNO under-registration (A dónde van los desaparecidos, 2023-11-09,
  https://adondevanlosdesaparecidos.org/2023/11/09/el-registro-nacional-de-personas-desaparecidas-y-no-localizadas-una-posibilidad-para-visibilizar-la-desaparicion/).
- Guerrero collectives' counts vs official counts diverge persistently (see Source
  Disagreement below).

### Fosas / Collective Search Context

Question: has the state had major clandestine-grave discoveries or search-collective
actions that could help explain why opacity matters locally?

Status: VERIFIED (2026-07-06). Guerrero is arguably the *birthplace* of the modern
buscadoras movement:

- **Los Otros Desaparecidos de Iguala** formed after Ayotzinapa (Nov 2014), when
  searching for the 43 students surfaced graves of people nobody was officially
  looking for; 160+ bodies exhumed, densest finds at Cerro Gordo, Iguala (HRW,
  https://www.hrw.org/es/news/2019/01/14/mexico-los-otros-desaparecidos).
- Tlachinollan credits women-led collectives — not state institutions — with locating
  260+ bodies in Iguala and ~70 in Acapulco.
- Active collectives include Familiares en Búsqueda María Herrera (168 sought),
  Guerrero No Más Desaparecidos (60 sought); the national Brigadas Nacionales de
  Búsqueda repeatedly deploy in Guerrero (4th Brigade covered by Vice). Searches
  continue (Semana Santa 2026 operations in Iguala/Buenavista de Cuéllar — Proceso,
  2026-04-03; during which eight families — five in Buenavista, three in Iguala — approached
  the searchers to report cases — Proceso does not say whether they were already officially
  registered (re-checked 2026-09-22),
  families bringing cases to collectives in real time).
- Stage-2 relevance: Iguala/Cerro Gordo find-events are strong candidates for the
  clandestine-finds dataset, with clear collective-led attribution.

Question: do other sources disagree with the official pattern: Data Civica, Red Lupa,
state registries, press reports, or local NGO datasets?

Status: VERIFIED (2026-07-06). Yes — but the disagreement runs the OPPOSITE direction
from Jalisco:

| Source | Figure | Note |
|---|---:|---|
| Our mirror (`data.csv`) | 4,453 unresolved | mirror snapshot |
| CNB (press, 2026) | ~4,500+ | consistent with mirror |
| Tlachinollan / state registry ref | ~3,800 | state-registered |
| Iguala collectives alone | 623 | three collectives combined |

In Jalisco the *official* registries disagree with each other (federal vs state);
in Guerrero the officials broadly agree with each other and *civil society counts
exceed them* — the gap is unrecorded people, not confidentialized records.

## Preliminary Case Question

What would explain why Guerrero has this combination of crisis scale and
public-registry opacity?

**Working answer (2026-07-06):** Guerrero's 9.1% confidential share does not mean
Guerrero is transparent — it means Guerrero's opacity takes a different form. The same
federal consent rules apply (its CEBP privacy notice mirrors the national architecture),
but there is no documented blanket-redaction policy like Jalisco's, and a meaningful
share of its caseload consists of historic grave-violation cases (Guerra Sucia,
Ayotzinapa) where máxima publicidad doctrine disfavors confidentiality. Meanwhile the
state's real registry failure is **omission**: collectives document substantially more
disappeared than the state registers, prosecutors are reported to discourage or stall
reports, and new cases surface at collective search actions rather than at the
ministerio público. Contrast with Jalisco: **redaction opacity (Jalisco) vs omission
opacity (Guerrero)** — a low confidential share can coexist with severe undercounting.
